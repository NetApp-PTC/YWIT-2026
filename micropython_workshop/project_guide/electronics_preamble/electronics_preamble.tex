\chapter{Electronics Essentials} \label{electronics_essentials}

This chapter covers the parts and circuit-building ideas you will use throughout the workshop,
from how electricity behaves to breadboards, resistors, LEDs, buttons, wiring, and safety. Later
projects add modules such as rotary encoders, speakers, and OLED displays, which connect the same way.

\section{Electricity in a Nutshell}

\subsection{Circuits, voltage, and current}
Electricity only does useful work when it can travel in a complete loop, called a
\textbf{circuit}: out of a power source, through your components, and back again. Break the loop
anywhere and everything in it stops. It helps to picture water in pipes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Voltage} (measured in volts, V) is the push, like water pressure. It is measured
    against \textbf{Ground} (GND), the 0V point that every circuit returns to. Your microcontroller's
    pins supply either 3.3V or 5V.
    \item \textbf{Current} (measured in amps, A, or milliamps, mA) is how much electricity is
    actually flowing, like the amount of water moving through the pipe.
    \item \textbf{Resistance} (measured in ohms, \si{\ohm}) is anything that limits the flow,
    like a narrow section of pipe. More resistance means less current for the same voltage.
\end{itemize}

A \textbf{short circuit} is a path from power straight back to ground with almost nothing in the
way. With no resistance to limit it, far too much current flows, and parts can overheat.

\subsection{Digital and analog signals}
A microcontroller pin can work with two kinds of signal:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Digital} signals have only two values. \textbf{HIGH} means the pin is at 3.3V
    (often written as 1 or \texttt{True}), and \textbf{LOW} means it is at 0V (0 or \texttt{False}).
    A button is digital: it is either pressed or not.
    \item \textbf{Analog} signals can be anywhere in between. A light sensor might produce 1.2V in
    a dim room and 2.8V in a bright one. Some pins can measure that voltage and turn it into a
    number your code can use.
\end{itemize}

\section{The Breadboard: Your Circuit's Foundation}

\subsection{What is a breadboard?}
A breadboard lets you build circuits without soldering: its grid of spring-clip holes grips
component leads and jumper wires to connect them.

    \centering

    \caption{The workshop breadboard with the Seeed XIAO ESP32-C3 microcontroller seated.}
    \label{fig:breadboard_overview}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Understanding the layout}

\subsubsection{Numbered columns (Terminal Strips)}
The main area of the breadboard is divided into lettered rows (labeled A through J) and numbered
columns (labeled 1 to 30). A hole is named by its row and then its column, so \textbf{A10} is
row A, column 10:
\begin{itemize}
    \item On the bottom half, holes \textbf{A}, \textbf{B}, \textbf{C}, \textbf{D}, and \textbf{E} in the
    \textbf{same numbered column} are connected together internally by a vertical metal clip (a \textit{terminal strip}).
    For example, placing two component leads in \textbf{A10} and \textbf{C10} connects them together because both
    share column 10.
    \item On the top half, holes \textbf{F}, \textbf{G}, \textbf{H}, \textbf{I}, and \textbf{J} in the
    \textbf{same numbered column} are likewise connected together.
    \item \textbf{Adjacent column numbers are NOT connected.} Column 10 is completely separate from Column 11.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{The center divider}
A plastic trench separates rows A--E from rows F--J. The two sides are \textbf{electrically isolated}
unless you connect a jumper wire across the gap. The microcontroller straddles this center trench so that
its bottom pins (in row D) and top pins (in row H) connect to separate 5-hole bus strips.

\subsubsection{Power rails}
Along the top and bottom edges run long horizontal strips called \textbf{power rails}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Positive rail (+, red line):} Used to distribute power (3.3V) across the board.
    \item \textbf{Ground rail (--, blue line):} Used as the common ground (GND) return path.
\end{itemize}
All holes along the same rail are connected horizontally across all columns. Once your microcontroller is seated, you
will run jumper wires from its \textbf{3V3} and \textbf{GND} pins to these rails to power your circuits.

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=blue!5!white,colframe=blue!40!black]
    \textbf{Breadboard summary:} Holes in the \textbf{same numbered column} on the \textbf{same side} of the
    center divider (e.g., A10 through E10) are connected together. Holes in different numbered columns, or on
    opposite sides of the center divider, are separated until you connect them with a component or jumper wire.
\end{tcolorbox}


\section{Resistors: Controlling Current Flow}

\subsection{What is a resistor?}
A resistor limits the flow of electric current. Resistors protect sensitive components (like LEDs)
and help create reference voltages for analog sensors. They have \textbf{no polarity}, so they work
either way around.

\subsection{Resistance values and identification}
Resistance is measured in ohms (\si{\ohm}) and kilohms (\SI{1}{\kilo\ohm} = \SI{1000}{\ohm}). The resistance
value is indicated by colored bands around the body of the resistor.

\section{LEDs: Lighting Up Your Circuits}

\subsection{What is an LED?}
An LED (Light-Emitting Diode) produces light when electric current flows through it. A
\textbf{diode} is like a one-way valve for electricity: current can pass through in one direction
but not the other.

\subsection{Polarity}
LEDs are \textbf{polarized}---electricity can only flow through them in one direction:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Anode (+):} The longer leg. Connects toward the positive voltage source (microcontroller GPIO pin).
    \item \textbf{Cathode (--):} The shorter leg. Connects toward Ground (GND). If the legs have been trimmed, look for the \textbf{flat edge} on the plastic rim of the LED casing on the cathode side.
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\maybeincludegraphics[width=.7\linewidth]{electronics_preamble/led_polarity.pdf}
\caption{Use either clue to identify LED polarity: the cathode has the shorter lead and the flat edge.}
\label{fig:led_polarity}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Current-limiting resistor}
LEDs have very little internal resistance, so connected straight between power and ground they let
through enough current to destroy the LED and possibly damage the microcontroller pin. That is why
you must \textbf{always connect a resistor in series} with an LED: the current travels one single
path, from the pin, through the LED, through the resistor, and into ground. This kit uses a
\textbf{220\si{\ohm}} resistor for the LED.


\section{Buttons: User Input} \label{button_basics}

\subsection{What is a pushbutton?}
A pushbutton is a momentary switch that completes a connection when pressed and breaks it when released.
The buttons in your kit have four pins arranged in two connected pairs.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
    \maybeincludegraphics[width=.65\linewidth]{electronics_preamble/button_illustration.pdf}
    \caption{Pushbutton pin arrangement: the two pins on each side are internally joined.}
    \label{fig:button_pinout}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Understanding the 4-pin connection}
As shown in Figure \ref{fig:button_pinout}, the two pins on the top edge are permanently joined internally,
and the two pins on the bottom edge are permanently joined internally. Pressing the button bridges the gap
between the top pair and the bottom pair. When placing the button on the breadboard, put the two pairs in
different columns and make sure your two circuit connections go to opposite pairs, so pressing the button
closes the circuit.

\subsection{Internal pull-up resistors}
When a button is not pressed, the input pin it is wired to is not connected to anything. A pin like that is
called \textbf{floating}: it picks up electrical noise and can randomly read HIGH or LOW. The ESP32-C3
microcontroller has built-in \textbf{pull-up resistors} that your MicroPython code can switch on. A pull-up
gently holds the pin HIGH (3.3V) while the button is idle. Pressing the button connects the pin to GND, which
pulls it LOW (0V). This means you do not need an external resistor to wire a button!

Because pressing the button makes the pin go LOW, this kind of button is called \textbf{active-low}: in your
code, a reading of 0 means ``pressed'' and 1 means ``not pressed.''

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=blue!5!white,colframe=blue!40!black]
    \textbf{Did you know?} An internal pull-up cannot replace the 220\si{\ohm} resistor used with an LED.
    A pull-up is a weak resistor (tens of kilohms) whose only job is to hold an input pin at a known level,
    and it is not in the LED's path at all. The 220\si{\ohm} resistor sits \textbf{in series} with the LED,
    so all of the LED's current flows through it.
\end{tcolorbox}

\section{Addressable RGB LEDs (WS2812B)} \label{ws2812b_basics}
Several projects use a small strip of three \textbf{WS2812B} LEDs, often called \textbf{NeoPixels}.
Each one actually contains three tiny LEDs (red, green, and blue) plus a small control chip. By
mixing different amounts of red, green, and blue, each pixel can show almost any color.

They are called \textbf{addressable} because you can set each pixel to its own color using just one
data pin. The microcontroller sends a stream of color values down the data wire. The first pixel
keeps the first color for itself and passes the rest along to the next pixel, which does the same.
In MicroPython, you set \texttt{pixels[0]}, \texttt{pixels[1]}, and \texttt{pixels[2]} to colors,
then call \texttt{pixels.write()} to send them all at once.

The strip has three pins: \textbf{5V} (power), \textbf{DI} (data in), and \textbf{GND} (ground).
The pixels have no fixed color. Whatever color you see is the color the code chose to send.


\section{Jumper Wires: Making Connections}

\subsection{What are jumper wires?}
Jumper wires are flexible insulated wires with male pins at both ends that plug directly into breadboard holes.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
    \maybeincludegraphics[width=.7\linewidth]{electronics_preamble/jumper_illustration.pdf}
    \caption{Male-to-male jumper wires in various colors.}
    \label{fig:jumper_wires}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Wire colors}
Your kit has two jumper wires of each of these ten colors: black, brown, red, orange, yellow,
green, blue, purple, grey, and white.

All jumper wires are electrically identical on the inside. However, following standard color conventions
makes your circuits much easier to read and troubleshoot:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Black / Brown:} Ground (GND).
    \item \textbf{Red:} 3.3V power.
    \item \textbf{Orange:} 5V power.
    \item \textbf{Yellow / Green / Blue / Purple / Grey / White:} Signals and data lines (GPIO pins).
\end{itemize}
A circuit with many connections may run out of a color. Extra ground or power wires can then
use another color.


\section{Safety Guidelines}

Working with 3.3V microcontroller circuits is safe and will not deliver an electric shock, but observing
a few basic rules will protect your components from damage:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Unplug before rewiring:} Always disconnect the USB cable from your computer before placing
    or moving components on the breadboard.
    \item \textbf{Avoid short circuits:} Never connect a jumper wire directly from the positive rail (3.3V or 5V)
    to the ground rail (GND). This causes a short circuit that can overheat the board.
    \item \textbf{Always use a series resistor with LEDs:} Directly connecting an LED between 3.3V and GND
    without a 220\si{\ohm} resistor will burn out the LED.
    \item \textbf{3.3V logic levels:} The ESP32-C3 GPIO pins operate at 3.3V. Always power modules in this kit
    from the 3.3V pin unless a specific project instructs otherwise.
    \item \textbf{Disconnect if hot:} If any component feels hot to the touch or you notice a burning smell,
    immediately unplug the USB cable and ask an instructor to inspect your wiring.
\end{itemize}
