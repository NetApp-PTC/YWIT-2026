\chapter{Red Light, Green Light}

\section{Overview}
In this project, you will build a reflex game with a button and a 3-pixel WS2812B LED strip.
Each reaction window cycles through red, yellow, and green phases:
\begin{itemize}
    \item During \textbf{RED}, do not press. Pressing during red ends the round immediately.
    \item During \textbf{YELLOW}, get ready for green and do not press yet.
    \item During \textbf{GREEN}, press the button as quickly as possible to score a point.
\end{itemize}

Each successful green press prints your reaction time in milliseconds to the serial console.
Each round has 5 total reaction windows. A hit during green scores a point, and a missed green
window counts as a miss.

\subsubsection{Place the components}
The LED strip has a right-angle header so it stands upright once seated. Push its three pins into
the top half of the board: \textbf{5V} into \bbhole{pixels.header.power}{G20}, \textbf{DI} into
\bbhole{pixels.header.data}{G21}, and \textbf{GND} into \bbhole{pixels.header.ground}{G22}.
Press the button into the bottom edge of the board so its legs reach from row \textbf{A} up to
row \textbf{D}. One pair of legs goes into \bbhole{button.side-a}{A12} and \textbf{D12}; the
other pair goes into \bbhole{button.side-b}{A14} and \textbf{D14}. The two legs of each pair are
already joined inside the button.

\subsection{Programming the microcontroller}
Open the file \texttt{project\_2\_red\_light\_green\_light.py} and run it in the IDE (see the \nameref{ide} chapter).

At startup, the serial console will print instructions and wait for a button press to begin:
\begin{verbatim}
Project 2: Red Light, Green Light
Press button to start.
\end{verbatim}

During gameplay:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The 3 pixels act like a stoplight. The pixels themselves can show any color (see
    \nameref{ws2812b_basics} in the \nameref{electronics_essentials} chapter); the code simply
    always uses the first one for red, the middle one for yellow, and the last one for green.
    \item RED phase lights only the red LED and prints ``RED! Do not press.''
    \item YELLOW phase lights only the yellow LED and prints ``YELLOW! Get ready...''
    \item GREEN phase lights only the green LED and prints ``GREEN! Press now.''
    \item Idle/round-over uses the yellow LED as a waiting indicator.
    \item A valid green press prints reaction time and increases score.
    \item A missed green window is counted as one of the 5 windows.
    \item A red/yellow press ends the round and prints final score + best reaction.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Hardware test checklist}
Use this checklist to confirm your circuit and code are working:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Confirm LED order on your strip: the LED closest to the header is red, the middle is yellow, and the far end is green.
    \item Press the button while the yellow wait light is on: the game starts cycling through red, yellow, and green.
    \item Wait for GREEN (green LED only) and press quickly: score increases and a reaction time is printed.
    \item Do not press during RED or YELLOW: pressing before green ends the round immediately.
    \item Skip pressing during one green window: verify it is counted as a miss and the next window starts.
    \item Repeat at least 5 rounds to confirm stable behavior and reliable button detection.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=yellow!10!white,colframe=yellow!50!black]
    \textbf{If something does not work:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{No LEDs light:} Check that the strip's DI pin in \bbref{pixels.header.data} is wired to
        GPIO 20 (\bbref{jumper.pixels-data.start} to \bbref{jumper.pixels-data.end}), and that power
        (\bbref{jumper.pixels-power.start} to \bbref{jumper.pixels-power.end}) and ground
        (\bbref{jumper.pixels-ground.start} to \bbref{jumper.pixels-ground.end}) are in place.
        \item \textbf{Pressing the button does nothing:} Check the jumper from \bbref{jumper.button-signal.start}
        to \bbref{jumper.button-signal.end} (GPIO 21) and the ground jumper from
        \bbref{jumper.button-ground.start} to \bbref{jumper.button-ground.end}. The two jumpers must land on
        \emph{different} pairs of the button's legs (columns 12 and 14). If the button is rotated a quarter
        turn, its always-joined legs connect GPIO 21 straight to ground and presses are never seen; turn it
        and try again.
    \end{itemize}
\end{tcolorbox}

\subsection{How the program works}

\subsubsection{States}
A game like this has to behave differently depending on what is happening: the same button press
scores a point during green but ends the round during red. The program keeps track of this with a
variable called \texttt{state}, which always holds one word describing the current phase. Code
organized this way is called a \textbf{state machine}: at any moment it is in exactly one state, and
certain events move it to the next one.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{|l|>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{5.2cm}|>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{6.4cm}|}
    \hline
    \textbf{State} & \textbf{What you see} & \textbf{What moves it on} \\
    \hline
    \texttt{idle}       & Yellow wait light & Button press starts a round $\rightarrow$ \texttt{red} \\
    \texttt{red}        & Red light         & Timer runs out $\rightarrow$ \texttt{yellow}; a press ends the round \\
    \texttt{yellow}     & Yellow light      & Timer runs out $\rightarrow$ \texttt{green}; a press ends the round \\
    \texttt{green}      & Green light       & A press scores; the timer running out is a miss. Either way $\rightarrow$ \texttt{red}, or \texttt{round\_over} after 5 windows \\
    \texttt{round\_over} & Yellow wait light & Button press starts a new round $\rightarrow$ \texttt{red} \\
    \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Each phase lasts a random amount of time, so you cannot simply count in your head and press.

\subsubsection{Timing with milliseconds}
\texttt{time.ticks\_ms()} returns the number of milliseconds since the board started. When the
light turns green, the program saves that number. When you press the button, it asks how much time
has passed since then:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption=Measuring your reaction time]
reaction_ms = time.ticks_diff(now_ms, green_started_ms)
\end{lstlisting}

Always use \texttt{ticks\_diff()} rather than plain subtraction. The millisecond counter eventually
grows too large and wraps back around to zero, and \texttt{ticks\_diff()} still gives the right answer
when that happens.

The same idea runs the timers. On entering a phase, the program stores a \textbf{deadline}, the time
when the phase should end, and the main loop checks it about 100 times a second:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption=Moving from red to yellow when time is up]
if state == "red" and time.ticks_diff(now_ms, phase_deadline_ms) >= 0:
    enter_yellow(now_ms)
\end{lstlisting}

\subsubsection{Reading the button}
When a button's metal contacts close, they bounce against each other for a few milliseconds, which
can look like several quick presses. The \texttt{DebouncedButton} helper in the \texttt{lib/} folder
ignores those extra bounces and calls your function once per real press.

\section{Review}
In this project, you combined timed logic, button input, and LED output to build a full game loop.
You practiced:
\begin{itemize}
    \item State-based programming (idle, red, yellow, green, round\_over)
    \item Reusing a shared debounced button library for cleaner input handling
    \item Using \texttt{time.ticks\_ms()} and \texttt{time.ticks\_diff()} for timing
    \item Providing immediate visual and text feedback to players
\end{itemize}
